SEPTEMBER–OCTOBER 2026 · A PERSONAL TRAVELOGUE

America,
one road at a time.

From Long Island to Houston — with a friend, a minivan, and room for the unexpected.

Come along for the ride ↓

Some trips are planned around destinations. This one came alive in the spaces between them: the ferry crossing, the roadside coffee, a local bar in a new state, the mountain road that begged us to slow down, and a landmark we found because somebody said, “Let’s take a look.”

My cousin Asheesh and I set out in a Honda Odyssey and crossed a remarkable slice of America. The itinerary took us through New England and Vermont, across the fields of Gettysburg, into Washington, D.C., through the musical heartlands of Tennessee, along the Mississippi at Memphis, and west through Arkansas and Texarkana into Houston.

We were not chasing a checklist. In fact, one of our habits became a kind of rule: whenever we entered a new state, we tried to stop somewhere local — for a drink, dinner, coffee or a snack. The stops made the map feel lived-in. They gave each place a flavour and made the drive itself part of the story.

The thread through it allDiscovering America one state, one local stop, one unexpected landmark — and one memorable meal at a time.

BEFORE THE MILES

New York first. Then the open road.

Before the road trip began, New York had already started to win me over. There was the Staten Island Ferry, with the Statue of Liberty and the Manhattan skyline appearing across the harbour; lunch and a beer at River Dock Café on a bright day; and the first ride to Times Square, where the city seems to put on a show simply by being itself.

I walked the High Line with Asheesh, through an elevated park that turns an old freight railway into a green ribbon above Manhattan. Washington Square Park had its own buzz — students, musicians and the energy of NYU spilling into Greenwich Village. Stone Street offered a different New York: historic cobbles, outdoor tables, and bars that made you want to linger.

These days in the city were a prelude rather than the main event. The real adventure began at Port Jefferson, with an evening ferry, an extraordinary sky, and the feeling that the country ahead was wide open.

THE TRAVEL JOURNAL

Eight days on the road

Not a race from A to B. A sequence of landscapes, local places, music, history and the small decisions that turned a long drive into a story.

DAY 1FRIDAY · SEPTEMBER 25LONG ISLAND · CONNECTICUT · RHODE ISLAND

A ferry, a crimson sky, and a double rainbow

  1. Roslyn Harbour
  2. Port Jefferson
  3. Bridgeport
  4. Providence area

We left Roslyn Harbour around 4:30 p.m. and headed for Port Jefferson, where our 6:45 ferry would carry us across Long Island Sound to Bridgeport, Connecticut. Before boarding, we stopped at Tommy’s Place for drinks and sides. A good road trip, we were learning, does not need to wait until the first overnight stop to begin.

Then the sky took over. The ferry moved beneath a crimson evening glow, with a double rainbow arcing over the water. It was the kind of welcome you could never put on an itinerary — and could hardly have designed better. We took photos, watched the colours shift, and began the drive with the sense that this trip was already giving us something special.

On the other side, we continued through Connecticut and stopped for dinner at Shell & Bones Oyster Bar and Grill around 9 p.m. By night, we were heading towards the Providence area for our first overnight stay. The journey had begun with water instead of traffic, and that felt like the right kind of opening.

The feelingA road trip that began with a little drama from the sky — and a reminder to leave room for the unplanned.

DAY 2SATURDAY · SEPTEMBER 26BOSTON · CAMBRIDGE · NEW HAMPSHIRE · VERMONT

Boston, Fenway, Harvard — and a northbound drift

  1. Providence
  2. Boston Common
  3. Fenway Park
  4. Harvard Square
  5. Colchester

We left for Boston late in the morning. The city offered several versions of America within a short drive: the harbour, Boston Common and its memorials, then Fenway Park, where the old ballpark feel is part of the attraction even when there is no game to watch. Boston Common is more than a pleasant green space: established in 1634, it is widely recognised as America’s oldest public park. Its paths and monuments place centuries of civic life within the modern city.

We crossed into Cambridge and had ramen at Santouka near Harvard Square. It was a satisfying contrast: a Japanese lunch in a university neighbourhood, all surrounded by brick, students and the sense of a place that has been thinking for a very long time.

Later, on the way north, we stopped for drinks at a Mexican bar in New Hampshire. We never pinned down its name — and that is part of the memory too. The stop wasn’t an attraction; it was simply a local place where we could sit, have a drink, and feel the transition from one state to another. We reached Colchester, Vermont, for the night with the Green Mountain State waiting for us in the morning.

The feelingHistoric streets and famous institutions, balanced by an ordinary bar stop that made the route personal.

DAY 3SUNDAY · SEPTEMBER 27VERMONT · LAKE CHAMPLAIN · SMUGGLERS’ NOTCH

Rain, mountain roads, and ice cream by closing time

  1. Colchester
  2. Burlington
  3. Shelburne
  4. Smugglers’ Notch
  5. Stowe
  6. Lake George

Vermont was the day for changing scenery. We drove into Burlington, passed the University of Vermont and went to the waterfront at Lake Champlain. Lunch and drinks at Spot on the Dock gave us a waterside pause; seeing a train arrive at Burlington station was another little scene that stuck in the mind.

We had planned a tour at Shelburne Farms, but the drizzle kept coming and we decided not to do the full visit. We looked around the gift shop, read about the property, and moved on. A plan doesn’t always have to work for a stop to count.

We stopped for drinks at El Gato Cantina, then took the road through Smugglers’ Notch and Stowe. The landscape tightened into a mountain pass, the drive became part of the attraction, and we reached the Ben & Jerry’s factory with barely fifteen minutes before closing. There was just enough time for ice cream and a quick look at the place where the company’s story became a global brand (the factory and visitor experience is in Waterbury; the company began in Burlington).

Then came another couple of hours behind the wheel to Lake George. It was a long day, but its best moments were not the ones that required tickets — they came from the rain, the road and the near-miss with closing time.

The feelingLet the weather change the plan, not the mood.

DAY 4MONDAY · SEPTEMBER 28LAKE GEORGE · UPSTATE NEW YORK · PENNSYLVANIA

A lake morning, an excellent coffee, and miles south-west

  1. Lake George
  2. Fort William Henry
  3. Carlisle

After the mountain drive, Lake George gave us a softer morning. The lake seemed to stretch forever, and there was time to walk, look around the steamboat area, and simply enjoy being beside the water.

Café Vero became one of those small discoveries worth remembering. The coffee was among the best of the trip, and the miniature coffee-brewing setup was interesting enough to photograph. I’ve included that detail below rather than leaving it only in the narration: the little brewing setup was part of the pleasure of stopping here. It was a tiny experience, but it captured the habit we were developing: look closely, even in places where you only planned to stop briefly.

We also visited Fort William Henry, at the southern end of Lake George. Built by the British in 1755 during the struggle with France for control of North America, it became a key outpost during the French and Indian War. Its most famous episode came in 1757, when the fort fell to French forces after a siege — a dramatic chapter later retold, with considerable fictionalisation, in James Fenimore Cooper’s The Last of the Mohicans. The site gave our lakeside morning a deeper historical layer: this beautiful waterway was once a strategic frontier. We left around 2:30 p.m. and headed through upstate New York towards Carlisle, Pennsylvania, where we stayed overnight. The day had fewer marquee attractions and more breathing room — a useful pause before Gettysburg the next morning. Fort William Henry Museum’s official history.

The feelingA reminder that one memorable coffee and a beautiful lake can earn their place beside a major landmark.

DAY 5TUESDAY · SEPTEMBER 29GETTYSBURG · WASHINGTON, D.C. · GEORGETOWN

Gettysburg: peaceful fields carrying enormous history

  1. Carlisle
  2. Gettysburg battlefield
  3. Washington, D.C.
  4. Georgetown
  5. Fairfax

We left Carlisle around 10:30 a.m. and drove through Gettysburg National Military Park. The battle fought here from July 1 to 3, 1863, became a major Union victory and a turning point in the Civil War. What makes this landscape affecting is the contrast: long fields and quiet roads, with memorials and markers explaining how fiercely this same ground was contested. The monuments are not decoration; they help visitors reconstruct the movements and losses that the open fields no longer reveal on their own.

At Abraham Trostle Farm, the farmhouse and barn stand as witnesses to the second day of the battle. The nearby “Unflinching Under Fire” interpretation describes the 9th Massachusetts Battery’s desperate stand on July 2, buying time for Union troops despite devastating losses. The field around it looks calm now; the plaque tells a very different story.

We also stopped at Little Round Top and read its historical marker, saw the artillery displays and the state memorials, including those dedicated to Pennsylvania and New York troops. The Gettysburg Address plaque and Lincoln statue added another layer: the battle was not only a military turning point, but a moment that shaped how the country understood sacrifice, equality and the Union itself.

The road then carried us into Washington, D.C. The switch was remarkable — from the fields where the nation’s survival was fought over to the capital’s monuments and museums. We visited the Smithsonian National Air and Space Museum, the World War II Memorial and the Lincoln Memorial. At twilight, the Washington Monument rose beyond the Reflecting Pool in a view we lingered over.

The day ended in Georgetown, with drinks at The Sovereign and Chinese food at Reren. We finished with a stop at George Mason University in Fairfax. A day that began among Civil War battlefields ended with a bar and dinner — precisely the mix that made the trip feel like ours.

The feelingGettysburg made history tangible; Washington made the national story visible.

DAY 6WEDNESDAY · SEPTEMBER 30BRISTOL · KNOXVILLE · NASHVILLE

From the roots of country music to neon-lit Broadway

  1. Fairfax area
  2. Bristol
  3. Knoxville
  4. Nashville

We left around 10:45 a.m. and pointed the car towards Tennessee. Bristol was an ideal stop along the way. We had soul food at Legit’s Kitchen, then visited the Birthplace of Country Music Museum, which tells the story of the 1927 Bristol Sessions. Over ten days that summer, producer Ralph Peer recorded performers including the Carter Family and Jimmie Rodgers; the sessions helped bring early commercial country music to a much wider audience. Bristol sits on the Tennessee–Virginia line, and the museum makes a persuasive case for why this small border city belongs in the story of a distinctly American sound. I even got into the spirit with a cowboy hat. The museum’s account of the Bristol Sessions.

In Knoxville, Awaken Coffee in the Old City gave us a break and another local place to experience. We crossed the Virginia–Tennessee line and carried on towards Nashville, arriving around 9:10 p.m. We were too late for the Bluebird Café show we had hoped to catch, but the night had a plan of its own.

We parked near Lower Broadway and stepped into a street where live music leaks out of almost every doorway. Robert’s Western World had the country-music atmosphere we were looking for — neon signs, musicians playing, a crowd cheering and dancing. We also stopped at Posty’s. A performer let me pose for a photo, and a vintage vehicle outside one of the venues added to the old-school Nashville feel.

The city felt alive well into the night. We stayed, listened, had drinks and soaked it up before finally reaching the hotel at around 2:30 a.m. Nashville was not a museum of country music; it was music happening around us.

The feelingWhen a plan falls through, leave the evening open. Sometimes the backup plan has a guitar and neon lights.

DAY 7THURSDAY · OCTOBER 1NASHVILLE · LYNCHBURG · MEMPHIS

A little country-music history, then a whiskey town

  1. Nashville
  2. Ryman Auditorium
  3. Lynchburg
  4. Memphis

The morning began at Ryman Auditorium. Having heard Nashville’s live music the night before, visiting the historic venue made the connection between the city’s present and its roots feel especially real. Opened in 1892, the Ryman became the home of the Grand Ole Opry from 1943 until 1974, helping carry country music from a regional tradition into American popular culture. We looked around, watched the history presentation and picked up a few souvenirs.

Then we drove to Lynchburg for the Jack Daniel’s Distillery tour, arriving around 1:45 p.m. The visit took roughly three and a half hours. We saw the Cave Spring, whose water is used in the whiskey-making process, the distillery grounds, the truck associated with barrel transport and the pristine office building. We also made the obligatory pose with a large chair and Jack Daniel’s signage.

It was interesting to leave the energy of Nashville behind and arrive in a small Tennessee town whose name travels around the world in a bottle. Lynchburg felt like a different chapter of American identity: craft, branding, and a place that grew around one very famous product.

We set out west afterwards and reached Memphis around 11 p.m. The day had already been long, and there was a lot to see the next morning — so we saved the blues city for daylight.

The feelingMusic and whiskey: two very different traditions, each rooted in a particular place.

DAY 8FRIDAY · OCTOBER 2 — INTO OCTOBER 3MEMPHIS · ARKANSAS · TEXARKANA · HOUSTON

Blues, barbecue, a river, a mill, and the final night drive

  1. Memphis
  2. North Little Rock
  3. Little Rock
  4. Texarkana
  5. Houston

We set off from Memphis around 10:30 a.m. with a packed day and rain in the forecast. We drove to Graceland, but after seeing the tour price and understanding that a proper mansion visit needed two or three hours, we chose not to take the tour. We browsed the gift shop instead. Sometimes the better trip decision is to keep moving rather than force a famous attraction into the schedule.

We visited the birthplace of Aretha Franklin, born in Memphis in 1942 and later celebrated as the “Queen of Soul,” then headed for Beale Street. The street’s music legacy is visible everywhere: stars in the pavement, B.B. King’s name, the Elvis statue and the venues that keep blues culture in public view. Beale Street became a major centre of Black commerce and music in the early twentieth century, and its clubs helped shape the blues that travelled far beyond Memphis. Even in light drizzle, it had an atmosphere of its own. We stopped at Charles Vergos’ Rendezvous and tried the famous dry-rub, charcoal-grilled pork ribs — a proper Memphis food memory.

At Mud Island Park, we walked beside the Mississippi River, looked at the Memphis sign, and read interpretive plaques about the bends and geography of the river. It was a satisfying pause: the river was both a landscape and a piece of infrastructure that helped define the city. The bridges across it made the connection to Arkansas feel immediate.

Then we headed west. North Little Rock gave us an unexpected stop at the Old Mill in T. R. Pugh Memorial Park. Built in 1933 as a tribute to Arkansas pioneers, it was designed to look like an abandoned nineteenth-century water-powered gristmill. Its faux-bois concrete bridges, benches and sculptural details—created by Mexican artist Dionicio Rodriguez—made it feel like a place from a fantasy film; I immediately thought of The Lord of the Rings. The mill appeared in the opening scenes of Gone with the Wind and is listed on the National Register of Historic Places. North Little Rock’s official history of the Old Mill.

We also saw Junction Bridge in Little Rock and read the story of La Petite Roche — the “little rock” that gave the city its name.

At Texarkana, we found the U.S. Post Office on the state line and took a photo that captures the idea of the journey perfectly: one of us on the Arkansas side, the other in Texas, shaking hands across a border. We had dinner at Verona before setting off again around 10:30 p.m. The Honda Odyssey carried us through the final stretch, and we reached Houston at about 3:30 a.m. on October 3.

The last day was almost a miniature of the whole trip: music, food, unexpected history, a beautiful river, a local landmark and the practical business of covering distance. We had set out to cross America; along the way, we kept finding reasons to stop.

The feelingThe miles were long, but every stop made the map less abstract. By the time we reached Houston, we had travelled through a string of very different Americas.

AFTER THE HIGHWAY

And then, Houston.

The drive ended, but the trip didn’t. In Houston, there was time to catch up with friends, share a meal at a local restaurant, and look back at how many landscapes and local worlds the road had stitched together.

We had travelled with a Honda Odyssey full of bags, snacks, photo stops and a constantly evolving plan. The car became part of the memory — the familiar base we returned to after battlefields, music venues, mountain passes and river walks.

For me, this was America not as a single image, but as a sequence: a ferry leaving Long Island under a double rainbow; New England streets and café counters; Vermont in the rain; Gettysburg’s peaceful fields; Washington at twilight; country music spilling onto Broadway; the blues of Memphis; a mill tucked away in Arkansas; and a handshake across the state line in Texarkana.

We came for the road. We remembered the stops.

Good company in Houston, after the miles.

THE ROAD TRIP, IN RETROSPECT

America was in the in-between.

The best parts of a road trip are not always the grandest. Sometimes they are a cup of coffee brewed with care, a bar in a town you had not planned to visit, a double rainbow that appears just as the ferry leaves, or a road rolling quietly through fields where history once turned.

We crossed states, chased closing times, changed plans because of rain, skipped attractions when the timing or price did not feel right, and kept driving. The journey was busy, imperfect and full of surprises — which is exactly what made it feel real.

One road, many Americas, and a photo album full of reasons to remember them.
